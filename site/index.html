        How the scoring works

        The Brier score

        Each settled prediction scores the squared difference between the chance you gave and what happened, counting 1 if it happened and 0 if it didn't. Your score is the average. Lower is better.

        • 0: you gave 100% to everything that happened and 0% to everything that didn't.
        • 0.25: what answering 50% every time earns.
        • 1: certain every time, and wrong every time.

        Glenn Brier proposed the score in 1950 to grade weather forecasts. His version adds up the error for both outcomes, which doubles every number. Hindsight uses the more common single-outcome form. Predictions marked void don't count.

        The three parts

        Allan Murphy showed in 1973 that the score splits exactly into three parts: calibration error, minus discrimination, plus uncertainty.

        • Calibration error (Murphy's “reliability”): how far your stated chances sit from how often those things happened. Lower is better.
        • Discrimination (“resolution”): how well your chances separate what happens from what doesn't. Higher is better.
        • Uncertainty: how hard the questions were. It depends only on how often your predictions came true, not on the chances you gave.

        Predictions are grouped by the exact chance you gave, so the parts add up to the score without rounding error.

        The confidence check

        Even a perfectly calibrated forecaster misses sometimes. For every prediction that leaned one way, Hindsight takes your confidence in the side you favoured and counts how often that side happened. It calls you over- or underconfident only when the count is more than two standard deviations from what your own stated chances predict. With a handful of predictions that rarely happens, so expect “no clear sign” until you've settled a few dozen.

        Why predictions are locked

        Once people know how something turned out, they overestimate how predictable it was, and often don't notice they're doing it (Fischhoff, 1975). The journal only works if the chance you wrote down stays the chance you wrote down.
